Mission 02 — World Shock Lab

World Shock Lab

How do markets, and Canada's in particular, react when the Federal Reserve or the Bank of Canada surprises them?

An open research project that measures how identified shocks move global assets, checks its estimators by simulation and by replicating published papers, tests in advance whether machine learning adds anything, and serves the results as a scenario tool.

Live site Code Research note

Problem

Markets move for many reasons at once, so a correlation between a decision and a stock index says little about cause. The project measures the response of world and Canadian markets to Federal Reserve and Bank of Canada surprises and to OPEC oil-supply news. It also asks whether the same shock matters more in some regimes, and whether machine learning forecasts announcement-day moves better than a linear model.

Every output is labelled as one of four kinds of evidence: identified causal, reduced form, model forecast, or hypothetical scenario.

Data

  • Fed surprises. The change in interest-rate futures in a 30-minute window around each FOMC announcement since 1990, split into a policy surprise and an information surprise.
  • Bank of Canada surprises. No free intraday Canadian data, so the project dates scheduled decisions from the Bank's press releases and uses the announcement-day change in the 2-year Government of Canada yield. Its variance on announcement days is matched control days (90% interval ), and after removing the same-day US move.
  • Oil and risk. OPEC oil-supply news, plus geopolitical-risk and trade-policy indexes labelled reduced form because they measure news coverage.
  • Markets. global assets. Yields and exchange rates from FRED, Canadian yields from the Bank of Canada, and prices for Canadian banks and country ETFs. Downloads are stored once, read-only, with SHA-256 fingerprints.

Method

  • Event-study local projections. For each horizon from the announcement through 20 trading days, the cumulative move is regressed on the surprise. In simulation the 90% bands contain the true response of the time. A zero-filled alternative manages , so it is only a robustness check.
  • Monthly macro effects. Local projections with instrumental variables, reported with the effective first-stage F and Anderson–Rubin sets, next to a proxy Bayesian vector autoregression.
  • State dependence. Each surprise is interacted with a regime measured beforehand. Wild cluster bootstrap p-values and Benjamini–Hochberg control cover the full set of regime tests.
  • Pre-registration. Splits, metrics, regime tests, and the rule for whether machine learning adds value were tagged in git before any model was fitted. The held-out test set was scored once.

Results

Canadian stocks move more on a Fed surprise than on a Bank of Canada surprise when each is scaled to a 10 basis-point move in its own 2-year yield. A second Bank of Canada estimator, Rigobon–Sack, narrows the gap. The direction of the Fed's lead does not depend on the method. published findings replicate, including that are not true almost by construction. Moved onto sets of random non-announcement days, no placebo is as large as the real Fed effects on the US 2-year yield and US stocks. headline responses keep their sign in every pre-registered robustness variant. shock-to-asset responses survive false-discovery control.

regime tests are suggestive before correction, and survive false-discovery control. The sample can only detect large regime differences.

Machine learning

Six models predict the 1-day and 5-day move of eight assets after each FOMC, Bank of Canada, and OPEC announcement, using only that announcement's surprise and the state of the economy before it. Validation years chose the linear projection and a neural network. The test set, announcements from 2019 to 2026, was scored once.

Limits

  • Machine learning did not improve on the linear model, and no regime effect survived correction.
  • The Bank of Canada proxy still carries some common North American rate news, so every Bank of Canada result is shown next to the Rigobon–Sack estimate.
  • Monthly macro effects rest on weak instruments (first-stage F of ), so they are labelled suggestive.
  • The proxy BVAR's bootstrap bands are too narrow in simulation. That is documented rather than tuned away.
