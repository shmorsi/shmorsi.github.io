Earnings Lens

Which sentences in an earnings release move the stock?

A natural language processing (NLP) study of 21,101 earnings press releases. It compares a fine-tuned FinBERT model with simpler text and number baselines on a time-based holdout, shows which sentences drove each prediction, extracts facts with a local language model that must cite its sources, and checks whether any of it survives trading costs.

Data

  • Collected 21,101 earnings press releases from 499 S&P 500 companies in the Securities and Exchange Commission's (SEC) Electronic Data Gathering, Analysis, and Retrieval (EDGAR) system: Form 8-K Item 2.02, exhibit 99.1, from 2016 to 2026, each with its exact filing acceptance time.
  • Stripped financial tables, boilerplate and contact details, and aligned each release with the stock's 3-day market-adjusted reaction, plus a separate tradable return measured from the first price after the release.
  • Split by date: training 2016–2021 (11,503 releases), validation 2022 (1,991) and test 2023 to September 2026 (7,607). Class cut-offs were set on training data only.

Models

  • Numbers only: standardized unexpected earnings and revenue growth from eXtensible Business Reporting Language (XBRL) data, using first-filed values only so later restatements cannot leak in.
  • Text baseline: Term Frequency–Inverse Document Frequency (TF-IDF) features with logistic regression.
  • FinBERT: fine-tuned in PyTorch on an Apple M1 graphics processing unit (GPU) on the 256-token opening of each release, with resumable, checkpointed training after GPU crashes. Validation log-loss was best after one epoch; the second epoch overfit.
  • Explanations: integrated gradients, implemented from scratch, highlight the sentences behind each prediction (mean completeness error 0.0037).
  • Fact extraction: a free local Large Language Model (LLM), Qwen 2.5 with 3 billion parameters running in Ollama, extracts guidance changes, revenue against expectations and against last year, and tariff mentions, each with sentence-number citations that are verified automatically and compared with a regular-expression baseline.

Results

Log-loss rewards calibrated probabilities. The area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) measures how well a model ranks releases that went up above those that went down; 0.5 means no skill. Confidence intervals are bootstrapped over announcement dates.

  • Text adds information beyond the numbers: adding FinBERT to the numbers-only model improved log-loss by 0.0182 (95% confidence interval 0.0133 to 0.0232).
  • Trading test: long the top third, short the bottom third, entering at the day-0 open and exiting at the day-2 close, over 5,072 positions. The best signal (TF-IDF on the opening) earned +22.3 basis points per position before costs (t = 3.31) and +2.3 after 20 basis points of round-trip costs (t = 0.34). The signal is real but small, and it does not survive trading costs.
  • Extraction: 97% of citations were valid at 15.6 seconds per release. Accuracy against my 200 hand-labelled releases is still being measured, so it is not claimed here.

What didn't work

Reported as found
  • FinBERT did not beat TF-IDF: on the same 256-token input its log-loss was worse by 0.0065 (95% confidence interval 0.0028 to 0.0100).
  • FinBERT's probabilities leaned toward "up" in the test period (average 0.39 for up against 0.31 for down); only its ranking was informative.
  • The local LLM found only 16 of the 45 releases mentioning tariffs (36% recall), so a simple keyword search beat it on that field.
  • A timing bug: Form 8-K filings made during market hours showed their price reaction on the filing day itself, because the press release came out before the filing. I corrected the day-0 rule; afterwards every filing-time group peaks on day 0.

Engineering

  • Evaluation with log-loss, AUC, rank correlation, bootstrapped 95% confidence intervals and a long-short backtest with date-clustered t-statistics.
  • An interactive Next.js and D3.js dashboard: live analysis through a FastAPI service locally, and a static demo on GitHub Pages precomputed for 200 test-period releases.
  • 20 pytest tests and a labelling tool for 200 hand-labelled releases.
